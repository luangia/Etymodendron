Etymodendron

    About Etymodendron

    Every English word in the tree comes from the ancient word or root at the centre — usually reconstructed Proto-Indo-European, the ancestor of most European and many Asian languages, spoken around 6,000 years ago. Older stages sit on the inner rings; modern English words on the outer ring.

    Reading the tree

    What do the colors mean?

    The family of languages a word passed through on its way to English:

    • Germanic — Old English, Old Norse…
    • Latin & Romance — Latin, French, Italian…
    • Greek
    • Other — Celtic, Sanskrit, Slavic…

    The colour is set by the first language after Proto-Indo-European, so patriot (Greek → Latin → French → English) counts as Greek.

    Why are some lines dashed?

    Wiktionary itself is unsure about that step: it says “possibly”, “perhaps” or “?”, or offers competing theories. Click the word to read the exact wording.

    What are the faint grey lines straight from the centre?

    Wiktionary names the root for these words but doesn’t say which languages they passed through. Rather than guess a route, the site draws none.

    What’s a “surprising cognate”?

    Two English words from the same ancient root that arrived by different routes: father is native Germanic, paternal came through Latin, patriot through Greek. Use “Highlight on tree” in the panel to see their routes side by side.

    What does the asterisk mean, as in *ph₂tḗr?

    The word is reconstructed: nobody ever wrote it down, but linguists worked it out by comparing its descendants. The odd letters (ʰ, ₂, ḱ) are the standard notation for its sounds.

    Inherited, borrowed, derived — what’s the difference?
    • Inherited: passed down naturally from parent to child language (Old English → English).
    • Borrowed: taken from another language (English took beef from French).
    • Derived: formed from another word in a way the source doesn’t spell out.
    • Formed from: built from parts, like a compound or a word with a prefix.
    What does the “+” after a word mean?

    Other English words are built on it: father + includes fatherhood, godfather and more. Click the word to see them.

    Missing or strange words

    Why isn’t my word in a tree?

    Only words whose ancestry Wiktionary traces back to Proto-Indo-European get a tree — about a third of everyday vocabulary. Words are left out when their history stops at Proto-Germanic, when they come from another family (coffee from Arabic, tomato from Nahuatl), when their origin is unknown, or when the Wiktionary entry tells the history only in plain prose the site can’t read reliably.

    Search still finds these words, marked “no PIE root”: pick one to see why, and where its traced history stops.

    Why does a word appear under two different roots?

    Compounds have several roots (television is a Greek part plus a Latin part), and some words have competing theories. Each is marked, and uncertain ones are flagged.

    Why are words like “reconstruction” hidden?

    Words that reach a root only through a common prefix or suffix (re-, -tion) would bury the interesting links. Tick “Include words formed with an affix” to show them.

    Accuracy

    Where does this come from, and is it reliable?

    Everything comes from the English Wiktionary, extracted by kaikki.org. If Wiktionary doesn’t state a link, it isn’t drawn, and every hedge in its wording is shown as uncertain. Wiktionary is written by volunteers and its quality varies, so treat this as a map to explore rather than an authority.

    I found a mistake. How do I report it?

    If the etymology itself is wrong, you can fix it on Wiktionary: it will appear here the next time the data is rebuilt. If Wiktionary is right but the site shows it wrongly, report it here.

    How up to date is it?

    It reflects a snapshot of Wiktionary; edits made since then aren’t included yet.

    Why does a Wiktionary link sometimes go to a missing page?

    Old forms are often written with marks (macrons, accents) that the page title drops, or the form has no page of its own yet. Those steps are labelled “no page under this form”.

    Using the site

    How do I get around?
    • Type a word in the search box (or press /).
    • Click a word for its full history; click an older form to put it at the centre — the trail at the top leads back.
    • Click a branch name to show only that branch.
    • Drag to pan; pinch or scroll to zoom. Small labels appear as you zoom in.
    Can I share what I’m looking at?

    Yes — the address bar always matches the current view, including the selected word. Copy the link.

    Can I reuse the data?

    Yes, under CC BY-SA 4.0: credit Wiktionary and its contributors, and share any adaptations under the same license.
